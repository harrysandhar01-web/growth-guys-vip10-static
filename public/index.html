Reader standard

How to evaluate a “verified” discount-code claim

The word verified does not have one universal evidentiary standard. A visitor evaluating a current discount-code claim can ask a few simple questions:

  • Is there a date attached to the verification?
  • Is the code that was actually tested identified?
  • Is the observed checkout result described?
  • Is there enough cart context to understand what was checked?
  • Is there a retained record behind the published status?
  • Can the current claim be distinguished from a general badge, testimonial, rating, or promotional statement?

VIP10 applies those questions to its own published results first. A verification label can communicate a conclusion; a structured record explains how that conclusion was reached.

Current evidence should be discoverable

VIP10 publishes important verification fields as visible page content rather than hiding a result solely inside a graphic or badge. That structure makes the record easier for visitors, search engines, SEO crawlers, AI systems, and other automated tools to interpret. The goal is not to ask a reader to accept the word verified; it is to make the evidence behind a status available for inspection.

Source desk

Start with the record, then read its limits.

The desk uses public primary records to teach source reading, not to make treatment, quality, or purchasing claims. A database record can help identify a study; a trial registry can show what was planned or posted; and a regulator page can identify an official category or notice. Each source has a different scope, date, and limit.

Readers can begin with PubMed for biomedical citation records, ClinicalTrials.gov for registered-study records, and Health Canada for Canadian official resources. The linked guides explain how to distinguish a source record from a conclusion it may not support.
