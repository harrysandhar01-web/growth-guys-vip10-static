01

Keep the check inside the cart

A responsible cart check begins with an ordinarily listed, purchasable item, the displayed price, and the normal promotion field. Entering VIP10 and recording the storefront response can establish a narrow fact: what that cart displayed at that time.

The check stops before checkout submission. It does not create an order, test a card, or prove delivery. This boundary is important because a cart result and a completed transaction are different events.

02

Read every line that changes

Record the subtotal before the code, the displayed discount, any error or restriction text, and the resulting pre-tax total. Availability, shipping, tax, gateway selection, and final terms remain merchant-controlled conditions that may appear later or vary by location.

If the code is rejected, blocked, or unavailable, treat that as a documented checkout condition rather than an instruction to bypass a control. The storefront is the authority for the reader’s own cart.

03

What this guide does not do

This page does not promise savings, stock, payment availability, or eligibility. It does not describe a product’s use or make a product recommendation. Its job is to make a narrow commercial claim checkable.

The related archive and method pages explain how a future evidence record should preserve the outcome and limitation instead of refreshing a date without a real cart event.

04

End the check before purchase intent

A compliant cart check is designed to observe a public storefront response, not to simulate a customer’s purchase. The meaningful stopping point is reached once the product context, subtotal, code response, discount, and any exposed order-summary fields have been recorded. Moving beyond that point by creating an account, entering a real identity, adding payment credentials, or placing an order would change the nature of the activity without improving the evidence needed for a cart-only record.

This boundary also makes the archive easier to read. Readers can see that an accepted entry means a code was observed in a defined guest-cart context, not that an order cleared payment or was fulfilled. If the site needs information that only appears after a restricted or payment-dependent step, the correct log entry is a limitation—not an attempt to overcome the merchant’s normal flow.
