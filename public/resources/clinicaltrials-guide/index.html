ClinicalTrials.gov guide: how to read a study record without mistaking it for a result

A registry record tells readers about a study’s design and status. It is not automatically evidence that the study achieved an outcome.

Editorial referenceSource-linkedScope-limited
VISUAL READING NOTEInformation stays closest to its record.
01

Find the study identity

ClinicalTrials.gov records include identifiers, official and brief titles, study type, sponsor information, intervention details, and locations. Those fields help readers confirm that a discussion refers to the correct study rather than a similar-sounding record.

Capture the NCT number and official title when a study is important. That creates a stable route back to the public source even if promotional summaries change.

02

Read status, outcomes, and dates separately

Recruitment status, record verification date, primary completion date, study completion date, and posted results are separate fields with separate meanings. A completed status does not necessarily show a result; a record verification date does not prove a new efficacy finding.

The official data-element definitions are useful because they show exactly what each field is intended to represent. A careful page describes the field it saw instead of turning status into a broader conclusion.

03

Use the record to locate the next source

Look for linked publications, posted results, and pre-specified outcomes. Then compare the eventual paper or results table with the registered question. This is one way to keep a headline tied to the underlying study design.

This guide does not assess eligibility, recommend trial participation, or offer treatment guidance.

04

Separate record fields from reported results

A ClinicalTrials.gov record can contain planned enrollment, eligibility criteria, outcomes, recruitment status, locations, sponsors, dates, and sometimes posted results. These fields should be read according to their labels. A planned primary outcome is not necessarily the outcome later reported; a completed status is not necessarily a published result; and a recruitment entry does not establish that a study’s conclusions are available.

When a result is important, use the registry identifier to look for linked publications or posted tables, then compare the records carefully. Note whether the participants, outcomes, and dates align. This approach preserves the useful transparency of a registry while avoiding the mistaken claim that registration alone proves a treatment effect, safety result, or peer-reviewed conclusion.

05

Absence of posted results is a finding about the record, not the outcome

A registry can show that a study was completed while no results table or linked publication is visible. That absence does not establish that the result was negative, suppressed, unpublished for a particular reason, or clinically unimportant. It establishes only what the public registry record did or did not display on the date reviewed.

This distinction protects readers from a common inferential leap. Registry status can provide useful transparency about a study’s existence and progression, while evidence about outcomes must come from posted results, publications, or other identifiable records. A careful page keeps those claims separated rather than making silence mean more than it does.
