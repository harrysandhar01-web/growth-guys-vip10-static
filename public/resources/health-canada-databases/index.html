Health Canada databases: how to find and interpret an official Canadian record

A Canadian database record can be highly useful, but the reader still needs to identify what that specific database is designed to show.

Editorial referenceSource-linkedScope-limited
VISUAL READING NOTEInformation stays closest to its record.
01

Choose the database that matches the question

Health Canada maintains different public resources for different purposes, including the Drug Product Database and recall/advisory information. A product listing, a notice, and an authorization-related record should not be treated as interchangeable documents.

Before summarizing a result, identify the database name, document date, product or record identifier, and the exact language displayed. This protects against turning a narrow database result into an overly broad claim.

02

Keep Canadian context Canadian

Regulatory status and public notices are jurisdiction-specific. A U.S. document may help answer a U.S. question but does not automatically state Canada’s current position. The reverse is also true.

A credible page links the official Canadian source when making a Canadian context statement and avoids adding a conclusion that the database does not itself support.

03

Use current agency records for current questions

Official records can change through new notices, updates, removals, and corrections. Readers should check the linked agency page rather than rely on a static excerpt. An editorial summary should show its source date and limit its claim to what was observed.

This guide is informational and does not provide regulatory, health, or purchasing advice.

04

Identify the Canadian record and its date before summarizing it

Health Canada publishes databases, notices, advisories, recalls, monographs, licensing information, and other official materials whose purpose and date can differ. A careful reader begins by confirming the exact database or document type, the product identity or category, the record date, and any scope statement on the page. This is more reliable than repeating a search-snippet description of what an official source supposedly says.

The source should also be read in its Canadian context. An official record can explain an agency category or communication without answering questions about another jurisdiction, a different product, a specific order, or an individual decision. The resource guide helps readers trace the original record and preserve those limits rather than treating every government page as a universal verdict.

05

Database search results require record-level reading

A result list can be a starting point, but the individual Canadian record contains the fields that determine what may be said about it. Read the record title, identifiers, status, dates, notices, and scope notes before carrying its language into a secondary page. Similar names, category labels, or partial matches can otherwise lead to a claim about the wrong record.

This record-level discipline also helps when a source changes. An updated advisory, an amended record, or a new notice may supersede a prior search result. The resource guide encourages readers to preserve the official link and date of access so the evidence trail remains checkable rather than relying on a snapshot of a search screen.
