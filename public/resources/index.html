01

Locate the record behind the claim

The PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov guides explain how to identify original literature and registry records. The Health Canada and FDA guides help readers distinguish regulatory information from promotional language. The recall guide explains why notices should be read for product, lot, date, and jurisdiction details.

None of these databases turns a search result into personal advice. The point is to improve source tracing and document reading.

02

Keep the editorial trail visible

The citation, change-log, glossary, and editorial-question guides give readers a repeatable way to inspect what a page says, what it links, and what remains uncertain. This site uses that same standard for its own research and cart-code material.

A useful resource library should give readers a route back to primary documents rather than asking them to accept a summary by itself.

03

Use the right public record for the right question

A publication database helps identify literature; a registry helps identify protocol and study-status information; a regulator page helps identify official authorization, safety, or recall context; and a merchant cart records a commercial response in a particular session. These records are not interchangeable, even when they concern the same topic.

The resource desk therefore organizes reading tools by document function. A reader should be able to move from a claim to the public record most capable of testing it, then note where the record remains silent. That path is more reliable than treating every search result as equal evidence.

04

Public resources work best when their limits are named

A database, regulator page, publication index, registry, recall portal, and editorial log each provide a different kind of public record. The value of a resource desk is not merely that it collects links. It tells readers which record is suited to a question and which questions that record cannot resolve. A registry can show a planned study, for example, while a result paper may be needed to inspect reported findings.

This orientation keeps readers from treating official-looking pages as automatic proof. The relevant task is to identify the source, read its date and scope, trace any linked document, and preserve the difference between what the record says and what a secondary page infers from it. The desk offers that method without providing individual medical, legal, or purchasing advice.
