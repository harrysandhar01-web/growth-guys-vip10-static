Overview

Short answer: A task number or report identifier alone is insufficient to establish that a particular container was the material tested. To connect a tested result to a specific vial you need multiple, independent linkages: a readable sample label, a batch or lot code that matches production records, an intact and uniquely recorded seal, a documented custody record, package-level identifiers, and documented delivery linkage. Each item serves a different evidentiary role; together they increase confidence but do not by themselves prove sterility, safety, efficacy, Canadian authorization, chain-of-custody integrity, or uniformity across a production batch. Conclusions must remain specific to the sample, method, matrix, and date examined .

Why this matters Laboratories and regulators often attach a task number to an item for tracking. That report identifier documents the laboratory’s activities and findings, but it is not, in isolation, evidence that a named physical vial was the one tested. Practical assessment requires examining how identifiers and records were created and linked. EURACHEM guidance on sampling uncertainty highlights that evidential strength depends on how the sample was handled and recorded from collection through analysis . Health Canada’s GMP guidance emphasizes record completeness and traceability as distinct elements of good manufacturing and testing practice .

A practical checklist for reviewers

When evidence is incomplete, explicitly state what is unknown and avoid extrapolating beyond what the records show. The strength of linkage depends on the combination and quality of these separate evidentiary elements; a task number is a pointer, not the proof itself .

  • What it is: A label physically affixed to the vial containing human-readable and/or machine-readable data (e.g., alphanumeric ID, barcode, QR code).
  • What it shows: The label can show a task number, product name, lot code, collection date/time, and collector initials.
  • How to read it critically: Confirm the label’s content against other records. Check whether the label’s format is consistent with known lab or manufacturer conventions and whether handwriting or printed text appears altered. A label alone is a visual indicator that ties the container to an identifier, but it does not prove that the label was applied at a given time or that it was not swapped later .
  • What it is: A production identifier assigned by the manufacturer to a group of containers produced under the same conditions.
  • What it shows: A batch code links a vial back to production records (formulation, filling line, date/time, equipment IDs).
  • How to read it critically: Verify that the batch code shown on the label or report matches manufacturer batch records or packaging data. Batch-level linkage can demonstrate origin but cannot demonstrate which physical container from that batch was selected unless the container-level unique ID is also preserved .
  • What it is: A physical seal, cap safety feature, or tamper-evident closure on the container or inner packaging.
  • What it shows: An intact seal indicates there has been no obvious opening since sealing; distinctive seal numbering can be used to link a container to a record.
  • How to read it critically: Photographs or contemporaneous descriptions of the seal before and after transport help. Note that an intact seal reduces the chance of post-sealing manipulation but does not eliminate other potential breaches; documented seal application and unique identifiers strengthen evidence .
  • What it is: A contemporaneous log of who handled the sample, when, and under what conditions (signatures, timestamps, transfers).
  • What it shows: Movement of the container through collection, transport, receipt, storage, and testing locations.
  • How to read it critically: Prefer records that include timed signatures, scanner logs for barcode reads, and temperature logs where relevant. Custody records create a chronological linkage between actors and locations but must be assessed for completeness and authenticity; gaps or backdated entries reduce evidential value .
  • What it is: Identifiers on packaging used for transport — airway bill, shipment number, courier tracking code, or pallet ID.
  • What it shows: Which shipment contained the container and the shipping conditions.
  • How to read it critically: Match package identifiers to courier records and arrival timestamps. Package-level evidence ties the physical movement of goods to dates and transport events, clarifying whether a specific container was likely present in a given shipment.
  • What it is: Proof of delivery or receipt records at the receiving laboratory or facility (signed manifests, electronic scans, photographic evidence).
  • What it shows: Arrival time, receiving personnel, condition on receipt.
  • How to read it critically: Compare receipt timestamps with custody logs and laboratory accession timestamps. Photographs of the package and inner contents at receipt provide an extra layer of documentation.
  • Seek independence: Prefer evidence from multiple independent sources (e.g., a xeroxed manufacturer batch record plus courier tracking plus laboratory accession log), not just multiple entries in a single system.
  • Sequence consistency: Verify that timestamps follow a plausible chronological order (collection → packaging → transport → receipt → accession → analysis). Inconsistencies, unexplained gaps, or overlapping times warrant follow-up.
  • Unique linkage vs. batch inference: Distinguish whether evidence links to a uniquely identified container (e.g., serial number + seal ID + custody signature) or only to a batch or shipment. Only the former can credibly claim the test result applies to that specific vial.
  • Examine contemporaneous evidence: Records made at the time events occurred carry more weight than retrospective notes. EURACHEM highlights that uncertainty grows when documentation is reconstructed after the fact .
  • Look for corroborating physical evidence: Photographs of the labeled vial, seal numbers, and package interior at each transfer point strengthen the chain of linkage.
  • No single document or identifier proves that the physical vial observed at analysis was the same vial produced or distributed earlier. That proof requires an unbroken, corroborated chain of unique identifiers and contemporaneous records.
  • Even with full documentation, conclusions are sample-, method-, matrix-, and date-specific. Presence of identifiers and records does not prove sterility, safety, efficacy, regulatory authorization, nor does it prove uniformity across other containers in the same batch .
  • Is there a unique container-level identifier (serial/serialized barcode) visible on the vial?
  • Does the vial label match manufacturer batch records and the laboratory accession?
  • Are seal identifiers recorded and illustrated at multiple handoffs?
  • Do custody logs include timed, signed entries or electronic scans for each transfer?
  • Do package-level shipping records and courier confirmations match receipt timestamps?
  • Are photographs or unaltered digital records available at key stages?
