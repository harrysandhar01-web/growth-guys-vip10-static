Source quality

How to source research without mistaking search results for evidence

A search snippet is a starting point—not proof. Strong research reading begins by finding the source, its date, its methods, and its limitations.

Editorial referenceSource-linkedScope-limited
VISUAL READING NOTEInformation stays closest to its record.
01

Start with the source, not the summary

When a claim is important enough to influence a decision, locate the original study, registry record, regulator notice, or systematic review. A blog post can help identify a topic, but it should not substitute for the document that actually contains the methods and results.

PubMed is useful for finding biomedical citations, ClinicalTrials.gov is useful for locating study records, and public regulator pages are useful for safety or approval context. Each source answers a different question; none should be stretched beyond its scope.

  • Check the publication date and whether later evidence exists.
  • Read who was studied, what was measured, and how long the study lasted.
  • Look for funding, conflicts, and limitations before adopting a conclusion.
02

Match the evidence to the claim

A laboratory measurement, an animal study, an early human trial, and a systematic review are not interchangeable. They may all be legitimate documents while supporting very different strengths of conclusion.

The most common quality failure is an evidence leap: a narrow finding becomes a broad consumer promise. A careful page shows the reader where the leap would occur and declines to make it.

03

Record the route taken to the source

A careful source search keeps track of how a document was found. A search result, social post, citation list, database record, publisher page, and full text can each be useful waypoints, but they are not equivalent evidence. When a claim matters, note the title, authors, date, identifier, and whether the reader reached the original record or only a description of it.

This audit trail helps catch a common error: treating a citation that resembles a study as though it contains the study’s actual methods and results. If the full text is inaccessible, say so. If a record has no posted results, say so. Accurate research literacy is not the ability to find a confident summary; it is the willingness to keep the source boundary visible.

04

Trace secondary summaries back to the underlying record

A news item, blog, database synopsis, or review may be an efficient way to discover a study, but the wording in a secondary source can compress caveats or combine several papers. When the claim is material, follow the citation chain until the primary research report, official document, or clearly identified synthesis is reached. Record which layer of the chain you actually reviewed.

If the underlying source cannot be retrieved, that limitation should shape the statement. It may be appropriate to say a secondary source reports a finding, but it is not appropriate to write as though the study’s methods and results were independently checked. The difference is small in wording and large in credibility.
