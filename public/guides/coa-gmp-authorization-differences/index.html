Overview

Short answer: they answer different questions because they are distinct kinds of evidence produced for different purposes. A certificate of analysis (CoA) reports test results on a specific sample or lot; a good manufacturing practices (GMP) benchmark describes whether a manufacturer’s systems and facilities meet expected quality controls; and a federal authorization record shows whether a product or its sale has met regulatory requirements for drug marketing. Each can inform the others but none substitutes for another: a single test result does not prove ongoing system compliance or legal authorization; a clean facility audit does not certify every batch; and a marketing authorization does not guarantee every shipment matches the approved specifications. Readers should treat them as complementary pieces of evidence and evaluate what each actually establishes before drawing conclusions.

Why they differ: purpose, scope, and basis of evidence

What each document does—and does not—establish

How to read and compare the three pieces of evidence 1. Check the unit of observation: CoAs talk about a lot; GMP benchmarks talk about a site and systems; authorization talks about a marketed product and its label. If you mix units—claiming a single CoA proves system compliance or authorization—you risk a category error. 2. Look for dates and scope: a CoA has sampling and testing dates and lot identifiers; inspection reports reference inspection dates and the scope of review; authorization records list approval dates, application types, and the authorized product details. Recentness matters, and older evidence may be qualified by subsequent events. 3. Match claims to evidence: ask what question you want answered. “Is this lot conforming?”—look at the lot’s CoA and any associated testing records. “Does the manufacturer reliably control quality?”—look for GMP inspection outcomes, corrective action histories, and quality-system documentation . “Is this product legally authorized to be sold in Canada?”—verify the product and manufacturer in federal records such as the Drug Product Database and read the authorization conditions . 4. Check for corroborating evidence: a CoA from a recognized laboratory, recent GMP inspection without unresolved findings, and an active authorization together form stronger, complementary evidence. But even together they do not prove absolute guarantees—sampling limitations, inspection scope, and post-authorization changes all leave unresolved possibilities. 5. Be careful with provenance and traceability: ensure a CoA identifies the laboratory, methods, lot number, and signatures; ensure the GMP assessment has clear scope and findings; ensure the authorization record matches the product’s formulation and manufacturer. Gaps in provenance weaken the evidentiary value.

Official sources and legal-advice caveat The guidance on GMP expectations and inspection basis is set out by Health Canada’s Good Manufacturing Practices guidance and related documents, which explain how system requirements are assessed . Guidance on the quality module and regulatory expectations for drug submissions is described in Health Canada documents that lay out chemistry and manufacturing requirements for new drug submissions and abbreviated submissions . The Drug Product Database provides searchable federal records of authorized drug products and their marketing status in Canada . These are official sources as of 2026-09-25. This article is explanatory, not legal advice: an analytical result or document discussed here does not by itself establish Canadian authorization or legality; only a regulator’s record and legal processes determine authorization status.

For a practical next step, compare the specific CoA, inspection report, or product record against the question it is meant to answer, then identify what additional evidence remains needed.

  • Purpose: A CoA documents analytical results for one sample or lot and is intended to show whether that sample met specified test limits. A GMP benchmark (inspection outcome, audit report, or compliance guidance) evaluates a manufacturer’s quality-management system, premises, and procedures against regulatory expectations . A drug authorization record in federal databases shows whether a product is legally permitted to be sold as a drug in Canada because its submission met dossier and regulatory review requirements .
  • Scope: The CoA is sample-specific and time-limited. GMP assessment looks at systems and processes over time. Authorization is a decision about a product’s dossier and label that applies to marketed product under specified conditions.
  • Evidence type: CoAs present laboratory measurements and acceptance criteria for a lot. GMP benchmarks rely on document review, inspections, and evidence of controls. Authorization relies on regulatory review of quality, safety, and efficacy data (as applicable) and the sponsor’s commitments .
  • Certificate of Analysis (CoA)
  • Establishes: the measured analytical results for particular tests (e.g., assay, impurities, microbial limits) on a named sample or lot and whether those results meet the stated specifications for that lot.
  • Does not establish: ongoing quality-system performance across time, regulatory approval, or that all future or past lots will meet the same results. A CoA is not a legal authorization; an analytical result alone does not prove a product is authorized for sale in Canada.
  • GMP benchmark / inspection outcome
  • Establishes: whether a manufacturer’s facilities, processes, and quality systems meet the regulator’s GMP expectations at the time and scope of inspection and according to the applicable guidance . It can identify systemic risks or good practices.
  • Does not establish: that every batch produced is conforming (inspections sample practices and records), nor that every analytical report is accurate. GMP compliance is preventive and system-focused; it reduces but does not eliminate the possibility of batch failures.
  • Federal authorization record
  • Establishes: that a product’s submission was reviewed and that a specific marketing authorization, licence or registration exists for particular conditions of use, formulation, and labelling. The Drug Product Database and related records indicate whether a product is authorized for sale in Canada and under what terms .
  • Does not establish: the quality of any particular lot in the marketplace at a given time. Authorization is a regulatory status based on dossier evidence and post-market conditions; it does not certify each physical shipment.
