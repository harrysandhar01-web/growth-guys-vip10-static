Overview

Short answer: To verify a laboratory accreditation claim you need specific, verifiable evidence of who accredited the lab, the exact certificate or file number, whether that certificate is currently valid, the physical location or legal entity the certificate covers, and—critically—the precise test methods, analytes and sample matrices listed on the accreditation scope. A logo or generic statement alone does not show which tests or samples were assessed, nor does it prove the claim remains current .

Why this matters Laboratory accreditation is not a blanket guarantee. Accreditation is granted to a named organization for specific activities and documented methods. The practical consequence: a laboratory may be accredited for one technique on one sample type (for example, measuring lead in drinking water using a specified method) but not for another technique or matrix (for example, measuring lead in cannabis oil). Seeing an accreditation seal without the underlying scope leaves the reader unable to judge whether the lab’s claim covers the exact test, analyte and matrix you care about.

What to verify, step by step Below are the concrete items you should look for and how each item changes what the accreditation evidence tells you.

1. Accrediting body (who issued it)

2. Certificate number or unique identifier

3. Current status and expiry

4. Legal entity and physical location covered

5. Test methods, analytes and matrices in the scope

6. How the scope is presented and how to read it

How to interpret what you find

Practical checklist to use when reading an accreditation claim

What remains unresolved by these checks Publicly verifiable accreditation scope confirms only what the accreditor assessed. It does not, by itself, prove overall validation beyond the listed methods and matrices, nor does it substitute for product- or clinical-specific evidence that may be required to demonstrate fitness for a particular use. Where evidence is incomplete or scope language is ambiguous, you should treat the accreditation claim as insufficient to establish broader assertions about performance or safety without additional current primary evidence .

A final note on evidence literacy Approach accreditation as a set of verifiable facts (who, which certificate, current status, where, exactly what was assessed). That approach converts logos and marketing claims into a clear checklist you can confirm against accreditor records. Doing so preserves the value of accreditation while avoiding overreach in what the certificate actually establishes .

  • What to check: the full legal name of the accrediting body (not just an acronym) and, where possible, a direct link to the accreditor’s public registry entry for the certificate.
  • Why it matters: different accreditors have different standards and registries. Public registries commonly allow independent checks of certificate status; a logo on a lab website does not substitute for the accreditors’ record .
  • What to check: the exact certificate number, file number, or unique registry identifier shown on the accreditor’s record.
  • Why it matters: numbers allow you to match the lab’s claim to a single entry in the accreditor’s database. Without it, you cannot be certain the claim corresponds to an active certificate rather than an expired or unrelated record .
  • What to check: the accreditation status (active, suspended, withdrawn, expired) and any listed expiry or review date on the accreditor’s registry.
  • Why it matters: accreditation is time-limited and subject to renewal and surveillance assessments. A historical certificate does not prove present-day accreditation. Public registries commonly list current status, which is decisive for a present claim .
  • What to check: the legal name of the laboratory (company or department), and the physical address(es) listed on the accreditation scope.
  • Why it matters: accreditation may apply only to a specific branch, building, or legal entity. A chain with multiple labs might hold accreditation for one site but not another. The accreditor’s registry typically specifies the entity and location tied to the certificate .
  • What to check: the detailed scope section that lists each authorized test method (often by recognized method code), the specific analytes (for example “arsenic, cadmium”), and the sample matrices (for example “drinking water,” “food,” “soil,” “cannabis oil”).
  • Why it matters: accreditation assesses competence for particular methods and sample types. The same analyte can require different validation for different matrices, and an accreditation that names a method and matrix is the only evidence that those precise combinations were assessed .
  • What to check: whether the scope cites standardized method identifiers (e.g., national or international method numbers), whether method versions are specified, and whether sub-scope notes clarify limits (detection ranges, sample preparation procedures).
  • Why it matters: scopes vary in granularity. A scope that cites a method without version or matrix information leaves unresolved questions about applicability. Conversely, a detailed scope that lists method versions and matrices permits more confident, evidence-based statements about what was assessed .
  • A current accreditor registry entry that names the lab, lists a certificate number, shows an active status, and includes the specific methods, analytes and matrices is direct evidence the accreditor assessed those combinations. It does not, however, by itself establish broader claims such as fitness for all sample types, clinical suitability, or product safety—those require separate primary evidence and context-specific evaluation .
  • If any of the elements above are missing from public records (for example, only a logo appears on the lab’s marketing materials), then the evidence is incomplete and the accreditation claim remains ambiguous. The absence of a detailed scope in an accreditor’s public record may indicate limitations on what can be concluded from the claim.
  • Is the accreditor named and can you find a registry entry for the certificate?
  • Does the lab provide a certificate number or unique identifier you can match to the accreditor’s record?
  • Is the certificate status listed as active, and is there an expiry or review date?
  • Does the accreditor’s record list the exact legal entity and physical location covered?
  • Are the test methods, analytes and matrices explicitly listed in the scope? If so, do they match the tests the lab claims to perform?
  • If anything is unclear, can the lab or accreditor provide direct verification (scanned certificate, registry link) that resolves the gap?
