Preprints and peer review: how to label preliminary research accurately

A preprint can make research visible quickly. Its speed is useful, but the publication status must remain clear: it is generally not a journal peer-reviewed version of record.

VISUAL READING NOTEInformation stays closest to its record.
01

What a preprint is

ASAPbio defines a preprint as a scientific manuscript uploaded by authors to a public server before journal acceptance. It may contain methods and data and may be updated through versioning, but it is typically posted without formal journal peer review. Those facts should appear beside—not underneath—the paper’s headline finding.

The important literacy point is not that preprints are worthless or that all journal articles are flawless. It is that publication stage is evidence context. A preliminary manuscript, a revised preprint, a peer-reviewed article, a correction, and a retraction have different editorial histories that a careful reader should be able to see.

02

Track version and peer-review status

When a page cites a preprint, it should identify it as a preprint and link the exact version used. If a later journal article exists, the reader should be told that the research may have been revised through peer review. A citation that hides the status creates an artificial impression of finality.

This is especially important for health-adjacent or commercially relevant claims, where early findings can spread faster than later corrections. A transparent site should prefer the most mature directly relevant source available and retain the preliminary label when a preprint is the only source.

03

Use status to calibrate—not suppress—discussion

A preprint can still be useful for understanding active questions, methods, and emerging debate. The appropriate wording is conditional: the manuscript reports, the authors describe, or the preliminary paper finds. It is not appropriate to state that a preprint has settled a clinical, regulatory, or consumer conclusion.

This page does not rate individual manuscripts or provide medical, legal, or policy advice. It establishes a publication-status rule for the library: show readers what version of evidence they are looking at and avoid upgrading a preliminary record into a claim of certainty.

04

Publication stage belongs beside the finding

A preprint can make a new manuscript available before journal peer review, which may be useful for following an emerging question. Its availability does not make it a journal article, and the absence of peer review is not proof that the work is worthless. The key is to label the stage accurately and avoid allowing a provisional document to carry more certainty than its methods and record justify.

When a preprint later appears in a journal, compare the versions rather than assuming they are identical. Title, methods, analysis, figures, authorship, conclusions, and limitations may change during revision. A careful page cites the version it actually read and updates its wording if the publication record changes, rather than leaving readers to infer that all versions say the same thing.
