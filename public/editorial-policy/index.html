Editorial policy: sources, claims, corrections, and commercial boundaries

A site earns trust by making its method visible. This policy explains what counts as a source, how claims are limited, and how material factual errors are recorded.

01

Source and claim standards

Material claims should be tied to the closest available primary or official source. A cart claim should point to a documented cart event or merchant-controlled checkout display. A research claim should point to the publication, registry record, systematic review, or official regulator document that supports the stated scope.

The editorial wording must not exceed the source. A one-item cart event is not a sitewide guarantee. A study in a defined population is not a result for every reader. A laboratory document for a sample is not proof about all inventory. If the source cannot support a claim, the site should narrow or remove the claim.

02

Commercial boundaries

VIP10 may appear as a subject of documented cart-code coverage, but the editorial library is not a commission-driven coupon aggregator. Conversion language must remain separate from evidence language, and information pages should not use medical certainty, fabricated scarcity, or universal-savings promises to pressure a reader.

The desk does not publish fake testimonials, false purchase confirmations, or claims of independent testing that have no underlying event record. A transparent limitation is preferable to a promotional statement that cannot survive close review.

03

Public-source and community evidence

Public discussions can provide useful historical context or identify a question worth independently checking, but they do not determine the current VIP10 verification status. Community posts, comments, forums, reviews, testimonials, social posts, and other public references are treated separately from the desk's own checkout evidence.

Public-source material can change over time. A post may be edited, a comment may be removed, a parent discussion may disappear, or surrounding context may no longer be visible. When enough original context is no longer publicly inspectable, the material is not presented as independently verifiable current evidence.

The current VIP10 status therefore does not depend on whether a community post remains online. A current verification label requires the site's own documented cart record.

04

Correction practice

When a material factual statement changes or is found to be inaccurate, the relevant route should be updated and the correction log should identify the date, the affected page, the nature of the change, and the reason. Silent rewrites may improve a sentence but do not create an auditable publication record.

Corrections do not erase historical context. The goal is to preserve a readable, honest history while distinguishing legacy formats from the higher evidence standard used for new records.

05

Keep source proximity close to the claim

Citations work best when they sit close enough to a claim for a reader to see what they are meant to support. A long source list at the bottom of a page may show that research exists, but it does not show whether any one source supports the sentence above it. The editorial rule is to make the connection between a claim, its document type, and its limitation legible rather than decorative.

The same principle applies to commercial observations. An archive entry should preserve the product context, subtotal, displayed discount, gateway state, and screenshot with the result it describes. Placing a broad savings message far from those fields invites overreading. Keeping the evidence adjacent to the claim makes it easier for a reader to test the page rather than merely trust its tone.
